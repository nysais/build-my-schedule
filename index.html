The attendee's view — one day, three states (speaking, registered, waitlisted)

What the attendee experiences

The flow is deliberately short. Anything longer and busy people don't finish it.

An email arrives with a one-tap sign-in link

No account, no password. The invite goes to the email they registered with; the link opens their private schedule page for this one event. The link keeps working for a month, from any device, so it survives a phone-to-laptop switch and inbox link-scanners.

They browse the schedule day by day and tap the sessions they want

Filters by track, format, and speaker help them narrow it down. If a session is full, they can join a waitlist and see their position. If two picks overlap, they get a gentle warning and choose. Sessions they're presenting are already checked and can't be removed.

Meal-block choices pair automatically

Conferences with a split-meal rotation (some attendees eat while others attend a session, and swap) are handled for the attendee. Picking a workshop in one slot auto-adds the meal in the paired slot. Nobody has to know it's a rotation.

They export it to their real calendar and to a printable page

An "Add to calendar" button hands them an .ics file that lands in Outlook, Google Calendar or Apple Calendar with rooms, speakers and times filled in. A "Download PDF" button gives them one to print for their notebook.

What it does for the conference

  • Real attendance forecasts. Room assignments stop being a guess a week out — the counts on each session are what people actually intend to attend.
  • Full sessions self-manage. Waitlists open the moment a room hits capacity; when someone drops, the next person on the list moves up automatically.
  • Presenters aren't chasing headcounts. The session leader sees who's coming, in a private list, without you cutting a spreadsheet for them.
  • The right people get nudged. Admins can send a follow-up only to attendees who haven't built a schedule yet — never everyone.
  • Fewer walk-ins to unbookable sessions. Attendees learn a room is full at the point they'd have added it, not at the door.
  • Check-in reflects reality. The badge, the print station and the analytics all read from the same list the attendee curated themselves.

How it's kept honest

Every seat is checked against live capacity, not a cached number, so two people can't grab the last seat at the same moment. Attendees can only see the schedule for the event they're registered for. Speakers can't be bumped from their own sessions by mistake. And a session that's been unpublished disappears from every attendee's schedule at once — no stale picks lingering behind the scenes.

For your IT team

Technical appendix

The feature is a small React page (public schedule) backed by an Express + Postgres API in pl-site. Sign-in is a stateless HMAC-signed magic URL, not a database token — bulk invite resends never invalidate a link an attendee already clicked. Session state is a two-column junction table (personal_schedules) with a unique index that also serves as the seat-claim primitive.

  • Magic-link email → HMAC-signed URL (30-day expiry, event-scoped, multi-use within window)
  • Capacity enforced inside a Postgres transaction; over-cap inserts convert to a session_waitlist row with a position
  • Meal-rotation pairing runs on POST /sessions and speaker-sync runs on GET /:eventId
  • Exports: .ics (RFC 5545) and PDF (PDFKit), both scoped to the attendee's own picks
  • Admin nag emails segment by invited but not built — never re-mails an already-scheduled attendee

Full write-up with schema, route surface, and code excerpts: github.com/nysais/build-my-schedule.
